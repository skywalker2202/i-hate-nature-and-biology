The core idea

We do not usually decide that a gene causes a disease from one observation. We build a case by combining several independent types of evidence.

Think of it as a chain of evidence: a variant is found in affected people, it follows the expected inheritance pattern, it changes the gene or protein, and experiments show that the change disrupts a biologically relevant process.

The more independent evidence lines agree, the more confident we become that the gene is truly related to the disease rather than merely nearby, correlated, or biologically interesting.

Evidence framework

1

Human genetic evidence

Are rare or damaging variants in the gene enriched in affected individuals? Do variants segregate with disease in families?

2

Inheritance pattern

Does the variant fit the suspected inheritance model—for example, recessive, dominant, X-linked, or mitochondrial inheritance?

3

Population frequency

Is the variant rare enough to plausibly cause a rare disease? A common variant is usually less likely to cause a severe rare disorder.

4

Biological plausibility

Is the gene expressed in relevant cells? Is its protein located in the right cellular compartment or involved in a relevant pathway?

5

Functional experiments

Does the variant alter protein activity, localization, stability, signaling, splicing, or another measurable molecular function?

6

Model organisms and rescue

Does disrupting the gene reproduce a relevant phenotype? Can the normal gene rescue the defect?

Variant discovered → Inheritance checked → Function tested → Disease mechanism supported

What each evidence type tells us

Evidence type Main question Example interpretation
Linkage analysis Does the disease track with a chromosomal region in a family? The disease locus may lie within a particular linkage interval.
Variant segregation Do affected relatives carry the variant and unaffected relatives not carry it? The variant follows the expected family inheritance pattern.
Population data How frequent is the variant in people without the disease? A very rare variant is compatible with a rare recessive disease.
Expression and localization Is the gene product present in disease-relevant cells and structures? ARL13B in cilia supports a connection to a ciliary disorder.
Functional assay Does the variant change a measurable molecular or cellular function? The variant reduces signaling, trafficking, or protein activity.
Phenotypic rescue Does restoring the normal gene improve the defect? This provides strong evidence that the gene is causally involved.

Worked example: ARL13B and Joubert syndrome

In a consanguineous family, affected individuals had Joubert syndrome and were homozygous for an ARL13B missense variant. Linkage analysis placed the disease locus in a region containing ARL13B. The variant was rare, altered an amino acid in the protein, and fit the known biology of ARL13B as a ciliary protein.

Additional functional and model-organism experiments supported disruption of ciliary structure or signaling. The conclusion came from the combination of evidence—not from expression or localization alone.
